Lesson 3 — the cheap scorers

Scorers that are rules, not AI. Free, instant, and identical every time they run. Most people skip these and pay a model to do what a line of code does better.

Start with the cheapest thing that works

You know the Berlin hotel cap is €210. The agent in s04 said €200. How little machinery does it take to catch that?

That is the entire thing. No model, no rubric, no judge. It runs in well under a millisecond, costs nothing, and will give the identical answer in five years.

The habit worth forming Every time you are about to reach for an AI judge, ask first: is there a rule that settles this? For a figure, a required step, an identifier, a format — there usually is. Judges are for things that need reading comprehension, and they cost money, take seconds, and disagree with themselves. Spend them where they earn it.

Three families, and they check different things

family 1

Structural rules

Read the shape of the trace, not the prose. Which tools fired, in what order, how many times, what arguments.

On our traces: submit_expense was called in s06 but the user never asked. Never called in s07 though the user did ask. Both are pure structure — no language understanding needed at all.
family 2

Reference match

Compare the answer to a known-correct answer. Exact match, substring, number with a tolerance.

Needs: reference_answers.json — the file you forced me to write. This family cannot run on live traffic, because nobody knows the right answer there.
family 3

Word overlap

Fuzzy similarity between the answer and the reference — how many words they share, in any order.

Why it exists: exact match breaks the moment correct prose is phrased differently. s03's answer is a paragraph, not a number.

What we already built without calling it a scorer

Look at the last three rows. The rule finds unsupported figures in all of them, and you passed all three — because of the distinction you made and I could not have specified in advance:

Your rule, from Lesson 2 Fail groundedness when an unsupported claim is load-bearing for the answer. Pass when it is incidental.

A rule can find the unsupported figure. It cannot tell whether that figure was load-bearing. That takes reading comprehension — which is the first honest argument for an AI judge we have hit, and it came from your own labelling rather than from me asserting it.

Your turn — you specify the rules

I implement exactly what you say, run it on all eight traces, and we compare it to your answer key. Be specific and binary. Where you are unsure, say what you would try and we will find out together.

Four rules, in words

  1. task_completion — how should a rule decide the user got what they came for? You have reference_answers.json. Think about what in the answer has to match, and how exactly.
  2. trajectory — what tool rules must hold? Think about submit_expense specifically: when is calling it correct, and when is it wrong? State it as a condition.
  3. safety — can a rule catch the s08 disclosure at all? What exactly would it look for? If you think a rule can't do it, say that and say why.
  4. groundedness — the existing rule flags any unsupported figure, which over-fires on three traces. Can you give me a rule that approximates load-bearing? A rough heuristic is fine — "unsupported figure appearing in the first sentence", or whatever you think tracks it.

Where a rule turns out to be unfixable, that is not a dead end — it is the specification for the judge you will write in Lesson 4. Finding the edge of what rules can do is the point of this lesson.
