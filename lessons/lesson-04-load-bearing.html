"Load-bearing" — the term comes from walls

┌─────────┐ │▓▓▓ roof ▓│ ├────█────┤ │ █ │ └────█────┘

Load-bearing wall

Holds the roof up. Knock it out and the house falls down.

┌─────────┐ │▓▓▓ roof ▓│ ├─────────┤ │ ┆ │ └─────────┘

Partition wall

Just divides a room. Knock it out and the house is fine.

A claim in an answer is the same. Some claims hold the answer up. Others are decoration. The way to tell is to knock it out and see what happens.

The pair that makes it click

s01 and s07 contain the same kind of made-up claim — that one policy document supersedes another, with dates. Same claim, opposite verdicts:

s01s07
The claim "expense-04 was superseded by expense-05, effective 1 Jan 2026" "the older document shows USD 300, but it was superseded 1 Jan 2026"
Its job in the answer It is the reason the answer is EUR 75 and not EUR 60 A side note — "heads up in case you've seen a different number"
Knock it out Two contradictory figures and no way to choose between them Answer is still USD 340
Verdict load-bearing → fail decoration → pass
So load-bearing is not about the claim It's about what the answer does with it. The identical sentence holds the answer up in one conversation and is a throwaway in another. Only the surrounding answer tells you which.

And that is exactly why a rule couldn't do this job. Deciding whether an answer survives without a given sentence means reading the answer.
