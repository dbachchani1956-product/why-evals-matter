Lesson 4 — asking an AI to be the judge

For the one thing rules couldn't do.

The exact sentence we need to catch

This is from s01 — the conversation you marked groundedness: fail.

what the agent told the user "The older one (expense-04-limits, effective January 2024) shows EUR 60… It has been superseded in full by expense-05-limits-updated, effective 1 January 2026."
what the search actually gave it — all of it Two copies of a table of meal limits by band, and one section called "Exceeding a limit".

The words superseded, effective, 2024 and 2026 appear nowhere in any of it.

The agent invented a document's status and publication dates. Your rule from Lesson 3 can't see it, because there's no made-up number in the headline — €75 was correct and properly sourced. The made-up part is a sentence.

Catching that needs something that can read.

What a judge is, mechanically

you write this, onceInstructions: what to look for, and what counts as pass or fail
+
the machine supplies this, per traceThe retrieved text, and the agent's answer
→
an AI returnsA verdict, plus its reasoning

That's the whole thing. It's a scorer whose logic is written in English instead of code. You already wrote four scorers in Lesson 3; this is the fifth, and the only difference is that the instructions get read by a model rather than executed by Python.

Which buys you reading comprehension — and costs you money, a few seconds per trace, and the fact that it can give you a different answer tomorrow on the same input. That trade is why Lesson 3 came first.

The instructions are called a rubric. Here's bad and good.

Both of these ask about something simple — was the answer concise? — so the form is visible without giving away anything about groundedness.

bad rubric

No criteria, no levels

Rate how concise this answer is,
from 1 to 10.
What you get back: "7". And next week, on the same answer, "8".

You cannot act on a 7. Nobody can say what would have made it an 8, so no one can fix anything. And the judge invented its own private idea of "concise" to produce it.
good rubric

Criteria, and what each level means

Decide whether the answer is concise.

PASS - every paragraph is needed to
  answer the question asked.
FAIL - the answer contains at least one
  paragraph the user did not ask for and
  does not need.

First list each paragraph and say whether
it was asked for. Then give one word:
PASS or FAIL.
What you get back: a list of paragraphs, each marked, then a verdict. Same answer tomorrow. And if it fails, you know which paragraph to cut.

Three things made the second one work:

part 1

Criteria

What specifically to look at. Not "is it good" — "is every paragraph needed".

part 2

Levels

What pass means and what fail means, stated so two people would agree.

part 3

Reasoning first

List the evidence before the verdict. A judge that answers first and explains afterwards is just rationalising.

The job: did the agent state something its sources didn't support?

You'll be given, for each conversation: the text the search returned, and the agent's answer. Write instructions telling the judge what to look for and how to decide.

  1. Criteria — what should the judge examine? (Think about what unit it should look at. The whole answer at once? Or something smaller?)
  2. Levels — what exactly makes it a pass, and what makes it a fail? You already have a rule here from Lesson 2 that you may want to reuse: unsupported claims matter when they're load-bearing, not when they're incidental.
  3. Output — what should the judge hand back, and in what order?
